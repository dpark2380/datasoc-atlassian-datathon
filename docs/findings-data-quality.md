# What we found in the data (plain-language version)

This explains, without jargon, what we discovered when we checked whether
the datathon dataset actually supports the story the challenge asks for.
Read this before building any slide that claims "X predicts Y."

## The short version

The dataset has two halves. One half is real and useful. The other half
looks real but isn't: it was generated randomly and doesn't tell us
anything true about customers. We built our analysis on the real half, and
we're treating our discovery of the fake half as a finding in its own
right, not something to hide.

## The part that's real

`product_usage.csv` (how much customers use Jira/Confluence/Trello/etc.
each month) is genuine data. Two things prove this:

1. A customer's usage level is stable over time. If someone was an
   active user in January, they were still an active user in May (the
   numbers line up 82% of the time). That's what real behavior looks like.
   People don't reset randomly every month.
2. Usage tracks the plan customers pay for, cleanly. Free-plan
   customers average about 5 active days a month. Standard: 8. Premium:
   10. Enterprise: 11.5. Higher-value customers engage more, which is
   exactly what you'd expect from a real product, not a rounding
   coincidence.

This gave us something honest to build on: which customers are using the
product much less than others on the same plan. That's a real signal for
who's at risk of not seeing value, and therefore at risk of leaving or not
renewing.

## The part that isn't real

The support ticket file (`customer_support_tickets.csv`) looks detailed:
16 columns, priority levels, channels, satisfaction scores, timestamps. But
when we tested whether any of it actually connects to anything, it didn't:

- The satisfaction rating customers supposedly gave is random. A
  Critical, badly-handled ticket scores the same on average as a Low
  priority, quickly-handled one. Statistically, it's the same as if the
  rating had been generated by a dice roll, because it was.
- Ticket type, priority, and channel are handed out at random, not
  based on anything about the customer or the issue. A customer's company
  size or subscription plan makes no difference to how many tickets they
  file, how urgent those tickets are, or how they're routed.
- The "time to resolve" timestamps don't make sense. In many cases the
  recorded resolution time is *before* the recorded first-response time,
  which isn't possible in reality. These fields were generated
  independently of each other, not from a real support workflow.
- There is no actual customer text to read. We hoped to analyse
  sentiment from what customers wrote in their tickets. That field doesn't
  exist in this dataset. The one text field that does exist
  (`Resolution`) is randomly generated filler text (e.g. "Case maybe show
  recently my computer follow."), not something a support agent wrote.

In short: you cannot use this file to learn anything true about which
tickets are worse than others, whether they were handled well, or how
customers felt about them. Any chart that appears to show a relationship
in this file (e.g. "Critical tickets have lower satisfaction") is
coincidence, not a real pattern. We checked, and the relationship
disappears in every case.

## Why we're presenting this as a finding, not hiding it

If Atlassian actually wants to use support ticket data to understand
sentiment and protect business value, which is the exact question this
challenge asks, the current data can't answer it, and that's worth
telling them directly. Our recommendation includes what would need to be
captured differently (real, ordered timestamps; the actual customer-written
text; tickets linked meaningfully to the customer's context) so that this
kind of analysis becomes possible in the future.

## What we built instead

Because the usage data is real, our analysis and recommendation are built
on which customers are underengaged relative to peers on the same plan:
bottom 25% of usage within their own plan tier, comparing a Free customer
to Free peers and an Enterprise customer to Enterprise peers, not mixing
tiers together. That's a legitimate way to flag accounts at risk of churn
or missed expansion revenue, using only the parts of the data that are
actually trustworthy.
